CAPITALONE CASE STUDY

CarWise — Powered by Capital One

A guided digital experience that helps first-time car buyers prepare for financing, understand pricing, and approach dealerships with more confidence.

ROLE

Research Lead · Survey, interview insights, and wireframes

TEAM & TIMELINE

Urwah Alpial, Angelly Tejada, Chunnu Ghimire, Asmaa Adi, and Mason Thomas · Applied Experience Design team project

TOOLS & METHODS

Figma, Adobe Creative Suite, Procreate, Notion, and Microsoft PowerPoint · Surveys, interviews, and secondary research

01 / CONTEXT

Making the first car purchase less overwhelming

Capital One designers challenged our team to investigate why buying a car feels confusing and intimidating for first-time buyers. Our research identified gaps in financing knowledge, realistic pricing, hidden fees, and negotiation preparation. CarWise was designed to bring guidance and transparency to those decisions.

The solution

Our team created CarWise to prepare first-time buyers before they step into a dealership. The concept brings financial preparation, understandable pricing information, and negotiation guidance into a supportive journey. It helps buyers explore what they can afford, identify potential fees, recognize dealership tactics, and approach sales conversations with greater clarity.

02 / RESEARCH

Listening to first-time buyers

Our survey received 110 responses, and we interviewed five participants, primarily students and alumni ages 18–23. I asked about financing confidence, dealership concerns, missing information, sources of advice, and the tools that could make buying a car less stressful. Findings centered on confidence, pressure, and support: buyers wanted clear financial education, negotiation guidance, and help making informed decisions independently.

  • 58% had never checked their credit score.
  • 78% felt anxious and unsure how to begin buying a car with a loan.
  • 74% were confused by auto and banking terms such as APR.
  • 90% were under 25, and 80% were students.

Survey findings

The survey gathered 110 responses. Alongside the findings above, only 20% of respondents felt confident about the relationship between credit scores and loans, and 57% had a source of income. Participants also expressed concern about being misled by sales representatives or loan terms.

Casual conversations

I spoke with students about their first car-buying experiences, what confused them about financing, and which parts of the dealership process felt overwhelming. I gave participants room to share their stories and followed up on what they knew about loans and pricing, what they had heard from friends or family, and what they wished someone had explained beforehand. These conversations helped reveal the emotional stress and knowledge gaps that shaped the early direction of CarWise.

Who we interviewed

Our five interview participants were primarily university students and alumni ages 18–23. Their experiences helped us connect the survey findings to individual concerns and decisions.

Interview questions

  • How confident are you in understanding financing terms?
  • What worries you most about visiting a dealership?
  • What information do you wish you had at the start?
  • Who do you rely on for advice when buying a car?
  • What tools would make this process less stressful?

Confidence

Many participants lacked confidence in understanding APR, loan length, and total payment expectations. This pointed to a need for simple, clear financial education.

Pressure

Participants feared dealership pressure and did not know how to respond to unfamiliar fees or add-ons. These concerns supported a design direction that included negotiation guidance and awareness of warning signs.

Support

Several respondents relied heavily on friends or family because they felt unprepared to decide on their own. This emphasized the value of helping users build independence and make informed decisions.

03 / PROCESS

Turning insights into a guided experience

Research informed our primary persona, Max Thomas, and a flow built around clarity, confidence, and practical support. We explored review transparency, price comparisons, dealership evaluation, and negotiation education through low-fidelity wireframes. Comparative analysis included Chase Bank’s Education Center, NerdWallet, and Reddit’s personal finance community. The team developed a visual identity using navy, red, and warm gold, with Montserrat and Esteban typography and Lucide iconography.

Define: our primary persona

Research revealed emotional and informational gaps around financing terms, hidden fees, and dealership pressure. We used those patterns to shape Max Thomas, a fictional persona that kept the team focused on clarity, confidence, and practical support.

Max is a 20-year-old, third-year student in Richardson, Texas, living off campus and expecting to graduate in spring 2026. He works part time, participates in campus organizations, and enjoys video games, comics, and music. Without a car, he relies on friends and family for transportation and sometimes misses events.

His frustrations include limited independence, feeling like an inconvenience to others, low confidence in dealership knowledge, procrastination, and fear of being pressured or misled. His persona statement captures that anxiety: “I’ve been thinking about buying a new car, but I’m scared I’ll end up paying too much for it, and getting scammed by the dealer.”

Low-fidelity wireframes

We explored the structure and flow before focusing on visual details. The wireframes mapped features identified through research: review transparency, price comparison, dealership evaluation, and negotiation education. Simple layouts helped us explore expert reviews, comparison tools, and learning modules, gather feedback, and refine the journey before moving to higher fidelity.

Brand elements

The visual identity aimed to communicate clarity, confidence, and trust. Deep navy and bold red conveyed stability, while warm gold added an approachable tone. Montserrat and Esteban supported readability, and Lucide icons provided a consistent visual language throughout the experience.

Comparative analysis

We reviewed educational and community resources to understand how they support car buyers and where the experience could be improved. The observations below reflect our project analysis.

Chase Bank Education Center

Strengths: Established brand credibility, links between educational content and product applications, and action-oriented tools such as calculators.

Opportunities: Content tied to the bank’s products can feel less neutral. The experience offered limited community interaction, and locating specific resources required additional browsing.

NerdWallet’s first-time car buyer guide

Strengths: Comparisons across financing options, detailed budgeting guidance, and links to calculators, lender reviews, and insurance resources.

Opportunities: Users move to other sites to apply for financing or buy a car, creating a fragmented journey. The guide does not itself provide an application flow or a conversational way to ask follow-up questions.

Reddit’s personal finance community

Strengths: Firsthand experiences, situation-specific questions, interactive discussion, and the ability to discuss sensitive financial concerns anonymously.

Opportunities: Advice can be inaccurate or unverified. Useful information may be difficult to find in long threads, making a structured and dependable learning experience valuable.

Key insights from secondary research

  • Preparation gives buyers more control. Understanding pricing and financing can help them navigate pressure and uncertainty.
  • Time and transparency matter. Clear fees and an efficient process can reduce frustration.
  • Financing belongs early in the journey. Comparing options and preparing before the dealership visit can make later decisions clearer.

Negotiation education

Our research materials explored requesting total “out-the-door” quotes from multiple dealerships so buyers can compare offers and identify fees. They also emphasized separating vehicle price, trade-in, and financing discussions rather than treating them as one bundled decision. These topics informed the learning content considered for CarWise.

Technology and culture exploration

We considered spatial computing as a way digital information can connect to physical environments, including examples such as augmented-reality headsets. We also explored Gen Z’s use of technology for social awareness and activism, alongside concerns about rising costs and economic uncertainty. These were exploratory themes rather than implemented prototype features.

The changing buyer experience

Our secondary research framed buyers as digitally engaged, practical, and financially aware. They often begin research online and care about quality, efficiency, features, and brand reputation. Long dealership visits, unclear fees, and confusing financing emerged as areas where a more transparent digital experience could help.

Features explored

  • Sign-in with a Capital One account, with a guest option.
  • Salesperson and car reviews.
  • A learning center for financial preparation and negotiation education.
  • Car comparisons across dealerships.
  • Customized recommendations.

These were ideas considered during ideation; the list does not imply that every feature was completed or launched.

04 / OUTCOME

A research-informed CarWise prototype

The team created a Figma prototype bringing together financial preparation, transparent pricing, and dealership support. Research outputs included survey findings, interview insights, a persona, and wireframes. The project culminated in a concept and prototype focused on the needs identified through research.

Prototype flow

The prototype reference shows a “Get Pre-Qualified” screen introducing a guided next step. The screen explains the information needed, presents the concept of viewing loan offers, and provides a Continue action. This is a description of the student prototype, rather than a live financing service.

05 / REFLECTION

Clarity, confidence, and practical support

The work highlighted that first-time buyers need emotional support alongside financial information. Their experiences shaped a design direction focused on understandable guidance and preparation before visiting a dealership. A next step would be to test the prototype with first-time buyers and use their feedback to refine the flow.
